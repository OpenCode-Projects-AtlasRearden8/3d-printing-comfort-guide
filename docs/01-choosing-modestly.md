# Chapter 1 — Choosing Modestly

The most expensive mistake in this hobby is not buying a bad printer. It is buying **too much printer** (or too much stuff) before you have printed your first useful object. This chapter is about choosing well and knowing when to stop — before what makers jokingly call collector's regret: a shelf of upgrades, a dozen spools, and three total prints.

## The one rule that saves the most money

**Buy enough printer to print small useful things. Do not buy a printer for what you might print someday.**

Let's be concrete. For a typical home, 95% of genuinely useful prints — clips, brackets, drawer dividers, hinge repairs, lids, hooks, spacers — fit comfortably on a bed about 220 by 220 millimeters (roughly 8.5 by 8.5 inches). That is the size of the classic "workhorse" class of printers, and it is more than enough.

Buying a giant bed because "I might want to print big someday" is the 3D-printing version of buying a truck because you might move once.

## The modest starter checklist

Use this as your list when comparing printers. Everything else is negotiable.

- **FDM, not resin.** FDM printers melt plastic filament and draw with it — this book's kind of printer. Resin printers cure liquid resin and produce gorgeous miniatures — and demand gloves, alcohol washes, and more safety overhead. Keep resin as a *later* hobby, not a first one. (Chapter 8 covers why.)
- **Bed around 220 to 250 mm.** Small enough to keep the machine compact and warm-ups fast, big enough for real household prints.
- **A removable, textured print bed.** Flexible steel plates that you peel prints off of turned a daily annoyance into a ten-second non-event. Anything else is a step backwards.
- **Reasonable community.** Choose a printer with lots of owners: more spare parts, more troubleshooting threads, more people who already hit your problem. Search for the model on big 3D-printing communities (r/3Dprinting, the maker forums) — a wall of helpful posts is a better spec than any marketing page.
- **Spare-parts availability.** Nozzles, belts, and build plates are consumables. Make sure they are cheap and easy to order for the model you are considering.
- **Some help with the first layer.** Either one-touch automatic bed leveling (a probe that maps the bed — Chapter 2 explains it) or a well-documented manual procedure. Both work; the automatic one removes the single most common beginner failure.

## What you do NOT need on day one

- **Multi-material (AMS/Tool changer) systems.** Wonderful for rainbow dragons and two-color signs. Overkill for every functional print in Chapter 5, and each material path adds a failure mode. Start single-color.
- **Enclosures.** Needed for ABS and high-temp materials that hobby homes rarely need. A shelf in a spare room does the same job for PLA (see Chapter 8).
- **A speed racer.** Printers that print extremely fast are genuinely impressive, but speed is the last thing a beginner should optimize. A calm, ordinary printer that finishes overnight for tomorrow morning is perfect.
- **The unpacking-bonus accessories.** Tool kits, dryers, extra nozzles, upgrades. After ten successful prints you will know exactly which one you need; buy it then. You will likely buy *one* tool and wonder what the other forty were for.
- **A second printer.** It *always* comes up. You will be tempted around print number forty. It is fine — but it is not a day-one decision.

## The honest cost picture

A modern, well-reviewed beginner FDM printer lands roughly in the 200 to 500 dollar (or equivalent) range today. Budget that. Then add the one essential extra: a few rolls of PLA as you go, in the 15 to 25 dollar range each. That is the entire realistic entry cost.

Things that inflate budgets in ways that are hard to justify *before the first print*: a second printer, a resin printer, an enclosure, a filament-drying station, four upgrade kits, and the forty-piece tool kit with the satisfying compartment case. None of them make your first print stick to the bed any better. Chapters 2 through 4 do.

## What actually comes in the box (and what to expect)

Most starter printers today arrive mostly pre-assembled: the frame comes together in an hour to three hours of calm, alphabetical screwdriver work, not a weekend. Include in your plan:

1. **Assembly or setup time:** an evening. Follow the printed manual (yes, on paper) rather than a random video library's version — the paper manual ships with the exact unit you own.
2. **A test print.** Most printers come with a pre-sliced sample file. Run it. It is not meant to be useful — it is meant to confirm the machine is alive.
3. **The first real print, which should be small.** Not a table centerpiece. A cable clip you actually need (Chapter 5). Useful failures on small prints are cheap; impressive failures on nine-hour prints are demoralizing.

## Framing the purchase: questions that predict regret

Answer these before clicking buy. If you cannot answer one, that is a sign to wait a week rather than upgrade your budget.

- **What will its first ten prints be?** (If you cannot name them, spend an hour in Chapter 5 first. Naming them now is the strongest regret-proofing there is.)
- **Where will it live?** A printer needs a home about the size of a microwave, out of reach of small children and curious cats, and near a plug (Chapter 8). "I'll find a spot later" is how printers end up boxed.
- **Who is nearby if it needs a part?** A model with a big owner community and fast availability of spares beats a slightly better spec sheet on a machine nobody stocks parts for.
- **Do you want to *use* things or *tinker with the machine?*** Be honest. Both are fine hobbies! But they choose differently: tinkerers enjoy machines with open firmware and upgrade paths; users should buy boring, proven, and closed. Buying the wrong kind for your personality is exactly how a printer ends up on a shelf.

## When to stop upgrading

You will know you have reached comfortable surplus when you can consistently say these three sentences:

1. "My first layer sticks, nearly every time." (Chapter 2, Chapter 9)
2. "I know why my last print failed." (Chapter 6)
3. "I own what I need for the next job, and I know what that job is."

After that, upgrades buy *nicer*, not more *useful*. Small chairs, faster print times, prettier finishes — fine to want, fine to skip. The regret-proof move is the same one that got you started: name a real object you need, decide whether the upgrade helps print that object, and let the answer decide.

## A note on "which printer" questions you will find online

Forums argue about printers the way car forums argue about engines — with passion out of proportion to the difference that matters to a beginner. Nearly every major, well-reviewed brand of FDM printer on today's market (Bambu Lab, Prusa, Creality entry-level, Elegoo, Anycubic, RatRig, and friends at various budgets) will print every object in this book successfully once you understand Chapters 2 and 3.

So the choice matters less than communities say. What you learn **on** the machine is the thing — which is why this book is about skills, not brands.
