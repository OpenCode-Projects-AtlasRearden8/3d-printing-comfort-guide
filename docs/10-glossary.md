# Chapter 10 — Glossary

Every term this book uses, defined in plain words and in alphabetical order. When a failure report or a community thread uses a word you do not know, it lives here.

## A

**ABL (Automatic Bed Leveling)** — A probe under the nozzle that taps the bed at several points so software can even out the nozzle's clearance. Handles the *distance* half of "bed leveling" (the tramming half is yours — Chapter 2).

**Anchor** — In slicer language, a decision point where a model is attached to the bed or supported. To a beginner: any point where something you didn't plan meets something you did.

**Axis** — A direction of printer movement: X (left-right), Y (front-back), Z (up-down).

## B

**Bed** — The flat plate the object prints onto. Textured, replaceable steel plates that flex to peel prints off are the comfortable default.

**Bed adhesion** — Whether the first layer grips. Poor adhesion is the origin of half the spaghetti in Chapter 6 — and Chapter 2 is its cure.

**Belt** — Rubber loop that moves the print head (or bed) along an axis. Loose belts cause layer shifts; tightening them is covered by your printer's manual and days of nerves saved.

**Benchy** — The well-known little tugboat test print. Not required to print; fun to try once you have a working first layer.

**Blob** — A lump of accumulated melted plastic on (or near) the hot nozzle that can bump, drag, or destroy a print as it passes. Cleaning it hot (with care) is in Chapter 6.

**Bowden (extruder style)** — A drive where the filament is pushed to the nozzle through a long tube instead of right at the nozzle. Simpler and lighter; more prone to some stringing quirks. Opposite: **direct drive**.

**Brim** — Extra flat flaps of the first layer reaching outward from a print's base, peeled off afterward. The everyday fix for small-footprint prints and lift-happy corners.

## C

**Calipers** — A precise measuring tool (often ~20 USD). Nice but not required; a ruler handles most home-print measurements (Chapter 5).

**Clog** — A partial blockage in the nozzle that makes extrusion thin and gappy. Cleared with a hot pull-through or a needle tool; spare nozzles are among the few purchases worth keeping in a drawer.

**Cooling fan** — The fan that blows on just-printed plastic so it hardens quickly. Slicers manage it; overrides are a later-hobby topic.

## D–E

**Direct drive** — An extruder mounted right next to the nozzle that pushes filament in directly. Handles flexible filament (TPU) better than Bowden.

**Dry filament** — Filament that is not carrying absorbed moisture. Wet filament fizzes, pops, and makes gappy surfaces (best answer: sealed bags plus silica — Chapter 4).

**Extruder** — The mechanism that grips and pushes filament into the hot end.

**Extrusion multiplier** — A slicer knob adjusting how much plastic per millimeter of travel. Rarely needed by beginners; mentioned on forums out of habit.

## F–G

**FDM** — Fused Deposition Modeling: the drawing-with-melted-filament printing this entire book covers. (Its sibling, **SLA/resin**, prints with liquid resin and is deliberately left for a later hobby.)

**Filament** — The plastic noodle (usually 1.75 mm wide) on spools that the printer consumes. See Chapter 4 for the whole PLA conversation.

**First layer** — The layer that sits on the bed, where adhesion problems and successes both live. Watching it print — every time — is Chapter 9's habit.

**Fan shroud, fan duct** — Small printed parts that steer cooling air. When they eventually break, printing their own replacement is one of the hobby's sweetest full-circle moments.

**G-code** — The numbered instruction file the slicer writes and the printer follows, one command per movement.

**Grid infill** — The default interior pattern in most slicers: crossing lines of plastic inside hollow walls, typically filled at 15% for everyday prints.

## H–I

**Hot end (hot end assembly)** — The heated block and nozzle that melt and push the plastic. Around 200 °C for PLA; not a thing to touch, clean, or fuss except as Chapter 6 says.

**Infill** — The interior pattern inside a printed object, expressed in percent. Ten-fifteen for everyday items, thirty-plus for load-bearing brackets (Chapter 3).

**Islands** — In slicer-speak, small disconnected parts of a model at some layer that would print unsupported in mid-air. Slide through the slicer's preview before saving; it catches these before they catch you.

## K–L

**Layer height** — How thick one printed layer is; 0.2 mm is the everyday default. The single biggest factor in print time (Chapter 3).

**Layer lines** — The visible horizontal seams of FDM prints. Part of the look of the hobby; sanding and finishing are strategies for later chapters in your own life, not in this book.

**Layer shift** — The whole print unexpectedly sliding sideways mid-run (loose belts, a snagged cable, the printer walking). The stair-step look; fixes in Chapter 6.

## M–N

**Nozzle** — The metal tip that draws melted filament, sized for layering (0.4 mm standard).

**Nozzle clog** — See **Clog**.

**PTFE tube** — Teflon-ish tubing serving as the filament path in Bowden printers and inside hot ends.

## P–R

**Perimeter (wall)** — The solid outline loops of each layer; the strength loan of a functional part (Chapter 3).

**PLA (polylactic acid)** — The plant-derived filament this book lives in; the easy, breathable, quiet default. All of Chapter 4.

**Print profile** — The saved set of slicer settings. Name your working copy; update one file, not a scroll — Chapter 3's advice.

**Raft** — A thick sacrificial base under the whole model. Effective but wasteful; try a brim first.

**Retraction** — The printer's small pull-back of filament when traveling between draws, to prevent stringy leaks. Tuned little by community profiles; touched only when stringing persists beyond moisture (Chapter 6).

**Resin printing (SLA)** — The liquid-resin family of printers: gorgeous detail, dedicated safety stack, a book of its own. Chapter 1 postpones it.

## S–T

**Skirt** — A loose practice ring printed around the object, unattached. Default-on in slicers; free insurance.

**Slicer** — The software that turns a model into a printed plan (Cura, PrusaSlicer, OrcaSlicer, Bambu Studio, and friends). All of Chapter 3.

**SLA** — See **Resin printing**.

**Spaghetti (print)** — The bird's-nest result of layers drawing into nothing after a detach. Everyone gets some; Chapter 6 is the calm owner's routine.

**Spool** — The reel filament comes on. Stored sealed and dry (Chapter 4).

**Stringing** — Thin web-threads between printed elements, mostly caused by wet filament. Chapter 6's third row.

**Supports** — Removable scaffolding under overhangs. You will need them for some models; most well-designed household models avoid them (Chapter 5 method).

**Thermal runaway protection** — Printer firmware that stops heating if temperature readings go inconsistent. A big reason Chapter 1 recommends well-known printer brands.

**TPU** — The rubber-like flexible filament for grips, feet, and cases (Chapter 4 table).

**Tramming** — Making the bed physically parallel to the printer's axes, with the screws underneath. First of the two jobs called "leveling the bed" (Chapter 2).

## V–Z

**V-wheels** — Round rollers some printer frames ride on; occasionally tightened as a maintenance ritual by your printer's manual.

**Warping** — Corners curling off the bed as a print cools. PLA's version is almost always first-layer adhesion, not plastic itself (Chapter 6, second row).

**Z offset** — The fine up-down trim of nozzle-to-bed distance, especially on ABL printers. Notes in Chapter 2.

**Z seam** — A small vertical ridge a print carries where one layer ends and the next begins in a fixed place. Slicers often hide it somewhere out of the way automatically; no action needed by beginners.

## Words this book deliberately left out

There are depths in every hobby — firmware flashing, Klipper tuning, flow calibration, gluing PETG, temperature towers beyond day one. They are wonderful when your hobby grows, and they are not required for a comfortable home printing life. When you eventually go looking, the community and glossary habit you built here will carry you without rewriting anything.

That is the whole book. Thank you for reading it — and enjoy the moment, somewhere in the next few weeks, when a drawer divider or a small hinge fix makes a stranger ask "you *printed* that?"
