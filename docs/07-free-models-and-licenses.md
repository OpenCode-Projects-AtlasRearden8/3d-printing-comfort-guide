# Chapter 7 — Free Model Sites and License Sanity

The 3D printing hobby runs on a remarkable gift: hundreds of thousands of files, downloadable free, made by strangers who wanted a drawer divider and figured yours is close enough. This chapter tours the good sources — and untangles the small print attached, because model files carry **licenses**, and a folder is not the legal wilderness some assume.

## Where the good free models live

Four large libraries do almost all the heavy lifting, in roughly this order for a home-use beginner:

| Site | Home-department notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Printables** (printables.com, by Prusa) | Strong functional-useful department, an active community of design contests and remixes, honest-labeled profiles. |
| **MakerWorld** (makerworld.com, by Bambu Lab) | Large functional section plus a big toy side; profiles and print settings often come bundled with the model itself. |
| **Thingiverse** (thingiverse.com, the elder) | The classic library; still enormous (a decade and a half of accumulated designs), some dormant uploads, occasional site quirks. |
| **Thangs** (thangs.com) | Search *by geometry* — you can match photos or parts and find a matching bracket. Handy for repairs. |

Between them, **search is the actual skill**. Use plain words a maker would tag: "cable clip desk", "drawer divider modular", "hinge replacement", "toothpaste squeezer". Searching what the thing does for you ("cable holder phone") often finds files better than product names.

Also worth knowing: **maker/designer personal pages** often link to their own libraries, and a search for the designer's name behind a model you already like surfaces their whole catalogue of similar, consistently-licensed work.

## The licenses, in plain English

Look — people do not read licenses, and artists know it. These three words are almost the whole system for home users:

### CC0 — "public domain. take it."

No conditions at all. Print, remix, sell even. There is no attribution obligation (and politely, nothing stops you from thanking the designer anyway in community comments — recommended and kind).

### CC BY — "take it, but name the teacher."

You may print, remix, share, and even sell, **provided you credit the original author** when you share or publish. In practice: when you post a photo of your printed drawer divider in a forum or personal page, add "design by [name], from [site]".

### CC BY-NC — "take it, personally only."

NonCommercial: fine for every object in this book — clips, dividers, hinge fixes, household helpers, gifts for the household. Do **not** print it to sell, or bundle into a product, or use it to pitch a business. NC is the freezing line for your young resale ideas; respect it.

Two less common marks worth recognizing: **CC BY-SA** (remixes must also carry SA: license your remix the same open way) and **ND, NoDerivs** (you may print exactly as-is, no modifications and no remixes). ND files print fine as-is; that is all you ever wanted from them anyway.

### When there is no license at all

Text saying nothing, or just a name? Under copyright law, unlicensed means **"all rights reserved"** — you may generally view and print privately, but cannot publish, remix, or commercialize without asking. In practice among hobby makers: many older models carry this status by neglect rather than by intent, and a short, friendly message often brings a polite yes. But the respectful default is simple: treat it as ask-first.

## The small habits that keep you sane

1. **Save the link with the file.** A photo of a printed clip does not tell you whose work it was. Save model page, designer name, license — your future thanks-something decides.
2. **Keep a "free to sell" filter in memory.** If someday you sell prints (farmers markets, Etsy), search within CC0 and CC BY only — most sites offer a license check box.
3. **Check before you remix and share.** A modification you share (the changed file) carries the same license terms — the name goes on, the NC stays if it exists.
4. **Do not re-upload other people's models anywhere.** Point people to the source instead. It keeps designers accessible and makes you the person whose links actually work.

Commercial selling from a licensed model is more a "yes actually" question — plenty of makers of these licenses are delighted by hobbyists and even encourage personal-income resale. But the polite, safe habit that solves every case: **read what the license says, and treat it literally.**

## A gentle note about copyright as a referee

The licenses above are **copyright-based instruments** that work much the same around the world. When your models come from public libraries and their terms are respected — and nearly every popular maker community is built on open sharing in the first place — wrecking your nerves over the legal details has no place in this hobby. Print your drawer divider with confidence.

## If you use paid marketplaces later (very optional)

Some designers sell files (site stores, Patreon-style subscriptions, small shops). Same habits, more rigorous: paid downloads come with their own explicit terms, seller or designer license page; keep your receipt emails if you might re-sell printed copies. It usually grants personal use plus limited resale counts; ask when in doubt. None of this touches the free-library life at all.
