# Chapter 4 — PLA Basics

If this guide could print only one chapter of the hobby, it would be this one — because for 90% of home printing, one filament does it all: PLA (short for polylactic acid). Easy to print, cheap, pleasant-smelling, fine indoors with normal ventilation, and strong enough for every object in Chapter 5. Learn it well and the other materials are optional scenery.

## What PLA is

A plastic derived from plant starch (usually corn or sugarcane), melted and drawn out into **filament** — a noodle 1.75 mm thick across most of the hobby, sold on spools. The printer feeds the noodle through a heated **hot end** where it softens, and the nozzle draws with it, one line at a time, until the object exists.

PLA's charm is that it is forgiving:

- It melts at modest temperatures (nozzle around 200 °C, bed around 60 °C — both within reach of any FDM printer without enclosure).
- It does not warp or lift off the bed easily.
- It cools fast, so details come out crisp, and it even smells faintly like food (a faint sweet note, not a burnt-plastic odor) rather than a stink you have to fight. Chapter 8 keeps its promises about safety at the right scale.
- It is stiff and dimensionally stable — clips, brackets, and drawer dividers hold their shape.

Its limits (know them, accept them, relax):

- **Heat** — a PLA part left in a hot car can soften and droop. Do not print PLA plant pot saucers for a sunny windowsill or a cup holder for a car-in-the-sun.
- **Water-outside** — PLA is not for marine anchoring or a hose fitting. It is fine for desk items and most dry-storage. It is not chosen for garden plants that are watered daily.
- **Brittleness over the years** — PLA objects can get slightly more brittle with time; big rubber-clamp-like parts may prefer a different plastic (later in this chapter).

None of these will affect a single useful object in this book. But they explain why "which filament should I use?" has a real answer: **PLA until a specific failure of PLA tells you otherwise.**

## Printing PLA simply

Your printer's default PLA profile will be close to right already. The working ranges you will settle into:

| Setting | Typical PLA value | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nozzle temperature | 195–205 °C | 200 °C is the classic; adjust in ±5 °C steps if a specific symptom appears (Chapter 6) |
| Bed temperature | 55–60 °C | 60 °C sticks better; cooler beds may release prints early |
| Layer height | 0.2 mm | see Chapter 3 |
| Print speed | default | speed tricks are an advanced hobby |
| Cooling fan | on from layer 2 | profile handles it; improves overhangs and surface |

Do not "improve" numbers before a print fails first. Every failure has a clearer fix than a temperature lottery.

## Storing PLA (spoil chases moisture)

PLA quietly absorbs humidity. Wet PLA prints with **tiny bubbles, pops, and a rough, fizzing surface** (the pop is the water boiling in the nozzle). Wet filament is the single most common cause of mystery print quality problems in the first year.

Storage that works for a normal house, in order of investment:

1. **The bag it came in, sealed, with its silica gel pouch** — free and adequate for the first months.
2. **Zip-top bags plus silica gel you bake back to life (or buy cheap)** — good for a year's collection of spools.
3. **A dedicated filament dryer** — genuinely nice, not necessary yet for most single-spool beginners. Buy one when you own four or more spools, or if the fizz appears persistently in a humid climate.

Keep spools off the floor in the room you print in — on a shelf, in their bags. A garage spool survives a humid summer by luck more than by design.

## The small art of handling filament

- Filament **kinks** if it jumps off the spool. Put your spool on a rack or in its spool holder so the noodle unwinds smoothly; a snag mid-print is one of the classic spaghetti causes in Chapter 6.
- Cut the very end of the noodle at an angle (flush cutters) before feeding — a flat-tipped end has a harder time threading through the nozzle's entry hole.
- When you finish a print session, **unload or tuck the filament end** so it cannot tangle; wrap the loose end around the spool and clip it into a hole (most spools have them). A spool left unclipped will wrap under itself and become a paperweight.

## The other plastics (know their names, use later)

You will see these in model listings. This is enough to recognize them, not to need them yet:

| Material | Strengths | Costs | When it wins |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **PETG** | tougher, more flexible than PLA, better outdoors, grippy surfaces | stringy, slower, occasionally finicky first layer | garden brackets, phone stands that will be sat on |
| **ABS/ASA** | strong, heat stoic, sandable and paintable | fumes that deserve ventilation, warping, needs bed heat | heat (cup-holders, decks outdoors), repairs to car parts |
| **TPU** | rubber-like — flexible, grippy | slow, needs a direct-drive setup generally | phone cases, gaskets, bumper feet, soft latch parts |
| **Resin (SLA)** microscopic detail | micro-detail minis | gloves, alcohol, real safety overhead (Chapter 8 rule: later) | figurines, jewelry masters, dentist-style detail work |

Rule of calm: **when PLA fails you at a specific job — it sags in the sun, snaps under bending, refuses to grip — you have a legitimate reason to try PETG (or the specific plastic that fixes it), guided by a search for "PLA vs PETG for [your exact use case]."**

## Disposal and the small conscience

PLA is not truly compostable outside industrial composting, so do not promise friends it will vanish in a topsoil — but it is at least plant-derived, and failed prints can go to regular waste or a recycler if your city accepts it. The larger honest answer: print things you actually need, and the waste question largely answers itself. A bookshelf of fifty dragons is a conscience decision, not a plastic one.

## The comfort loop (why this chapter is short)

PLA is nice precisely because **the settings are quiet**. One spool in a room-temperature corner, one profile, standard 0.2 mm, 15% infill, done. This is the hobby's baseline of calm.
